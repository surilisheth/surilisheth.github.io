Curriculum Vitae

I am an ETH Zurich Postdoctoral Fellow in the International Relations and Data Science Lab. I received my PhD in Political Science from the University of California, Berkeley, and hold an MPA/ID from the Harvard Kennedy School. I am also a Research Associate at the Center on the Politics of Development.

I specialize in the comparative politics of development, legal institutions, and violence with regional focuses on India and the United States. One strand of my research examines the ways in which marginalized citizens make claims on both state and societal institutions. I examine quotidian and ubiquitous cases of violence against women, marginalized castes, and tribal communities in India such as domestic violence and land disposession. In a second strand of my work, I study immigrants vis-a-vis inequality, intolerance, and carceral institutions in the United States.

Methodologically, I combine fieldwork with experiments, micro-surveys, large-scale administrative datasets, and nationally representative surveys. I also compile and analyze original archival sources such as domestic violence case files, vernacular newspapers, and caste council constitutions.

Job Market Paper

The Dependency Trap: Domestic Violence and Institutional Demand in Rural India

Abstract

Existing comparative politics scholarship reaches a surprising conclusion: legal reform and expanded policing have limited effects on women's reporting behavior of domestic violence. I argue that this is because potential claimants face a dependency trap: the condition in which people seek redress from institutions that simultaneously reproduce the unequal relationships on which their survival depends. Authority over marriage is distributed across state and social authorities: state institutions govern legal entitlements of separation, while kinship and caste institutions regulate its social legitimacy. Due to this fragmented authority structure, no single institution can enable the conditions for exit. Using an ethnographically motivated vignette experiment and an original archive of over 350 domestic violence case files from a grassroots organization in rural Gujarat, India, I find that married women's institutional demands are oriented not toward enforcement alone when they want to exit, but toward actors that can coordinate across these authorities. The findings imply that institutional demand under dependency is shaped not only by expectations of responsiveness to harm, but also by whether institutions can reorganize dependence itself.

Submitted and Working Papers

Missing and Miscoded Women: A Note on Measuring Domestic Violence Claims

Abstract

Why do many cases of domestic violence never appear in official data? Existing accounts emphasize barriers to reporting or institutional failure. Using the case of rural Gujarat, I offer an alternative explanation: observed reporting patterns can also reflect strategic institutional choice under constraint. Drawing on an original archive of 368 case files from a local women’s organization, I develop a coding framework to trace how survivors engage institutions and how these pathways shape measurement. I show that survivors’ relational objectives generate two forms of measurement error: cases that are missing from official domestic violence data and cases that are miscoded under alternative legal categories. Rather than seeking punishment, survivors use institutions to negotiate, exert pressure, or secure material support within ongoing marital relationships. These pathways systematically shape how cases are recorded across institutional settings. Using administrative data alone therefore risks conflating barriers to access with women’s strategic engagement with institutions, obscuring how agency is exercised through relational objectives under constraint.

Do Virtual Museums Highlighting the Experiences of Minorities Persuade Visitors? Evidence From a Study on Bias Toward Asian Americans (SSRN.)
With Charles Crabtree, John B. Holbein, and Cecilia Mo
Under Review

Abstract

Biases against individuals of Asian descent persist in the United States. Can cultural history museums, which aim to provide public education on the struggles, achievements, and cultural traditions of different groups, reduce these biases? We address this question through an experiment using virtual museum content curated by the Museum of Chinese in America (MOCA). By embedding MOCA’s virtual exhibits into surveys, we test whether exposure to the lived experiences and history of the Chinese diaspora in the United States alters non-Asian Americans’ attitudes toward and knowledge about Asian Americans. Our findings suggest that historical and emotionally resonant messaging common in museum exhibits improves attitudes and beliefs, primarily among individuals who already hold positive racial predispositions. These results highlight both the promise and limits of museum-based interventions aimed at reducing out-group bias and speak to the efficacy of scalable digital interventions.

Commonality or Difference? Sexual Assault and the Foundations of Women's Solidarities in the United States

Abstract

How does emphasizing differences within a social group affect group solidarity and political behavior? I examine this question in an online survey experiment fielded amid the 2020 racial justice protests following the killing of George Floyd, a period in which racial inequality and the politics of policing were unusually salient. The experiment varies whether sexual assault is framed as an issue affecting women generally or women of color specifically. Both frames increase women’s solidarity, contrary to the expectation that emphasizing cross-cutting racial identities fragments group cohesion. Yet they generate distinct forms of solidarity. The gender-only frame activates what I call prototypical solidarity, while the intersectional frame increases feelings of commonality with and closeness to women of color among both White women and women of color, producing intersectional solidarity. The two frames also have different political consequences: the gender-only frame increases engagement with a Violence Against Women Act petition, an effect driven primarily by White women, whereas the intersectional frame increases support for racial and gender descriptive representation but not engagement with the VAWA petition. These findings show that intragroup differences need not undermine political solidarity, but can shape its content and political consequences. More broadly, the political significance of intersectionality is not simply that people possess multiple identities: invoking intragroup difference can change the content of perceived commonality without weakening identification with the broader group.
