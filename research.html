The Dependency Trap: Domestic Violence and Claim-Making in India

Abstract

Existing comparative politics research finds that legal reform and expanded policing have had limited effects on women’s reporting of domestic violence. Building on feminist political theory, I argue that claimants may face a dependency trap: seeking redress from institutions that reproduce the unequal relationships on which their survival depends. This generates demand for intermediaries that can reduce violence without sacrificing agency. Drawing on an ethnographically motivated vignette experiment, I show that women seeking to leave abusive marriages are more likely to turn to local women’s organizations. Text-as-data analysis of open-ended responses and an original archive of 564 domestic violence case files provides suggestive evidence for a key mechanism: navigational capacity, or the ability to help women navigate multiple institutions while renegotiating the relationships on which they depend. Institutional demand under dependency therefore reflects not only expected protection from harm, but also whether institutions can help reshape the dependence underlying it.

Missing and Miscoded Women: The Institutional Trajectories of Domestic Violence Claims

Abstract

Why do many cases of domestic violence never appear in official data? Existing accounts emphasize barriers to reporting or institutional failure. Using the case of rural Gujarat, I offer an alternative explanation: observed reporting patterns can also reflect strategic institutional choice under constraint. Drawing on an original archive of case files from a local women’s organization (2012–2023), I develop a coding framework to trace how survivors engage institutions and how these pathways shape measurement. I show that survivors’ relational objectives generate two forms of measurement error: cases that are missing from official domestic violence data and cases that are miscoded under alternative legal categories. Rather than seeking punishment, survivors use institutions to negotiate, exert pressure, or secure material support within ongoing marital relationships. These pathways systematically shape how cases are recorded across institutional settings. Using administrative data alone therefore risks conflating barriers to access with women’s strategic engagement with institutions, obscuring how agency is exercised through relational objectives under constraint.

Do Virtual Museums Highlighting the Experiences of Minorities Persuade Visitors? Evidence From a Study on Bias Toward Asian Americans (SSRN)
With Charles Crabtree, John B. Holbein, and Cecilia Mo
Under review

Abstract

Biases against individuals of Asian descent persist in the United States. Can cultural history museums, which aim to provide public education on the struggles, achievements, and cultural traditions of different groups, reduce these biases? We address this question through an experiment using virtual museum content curated by the Museum of Chinese in America (MOCA). By embedding MOCA’s virtual exhibits into surveys, we test whether exposure to the lived experiences and history of the Chinese diaspora in the United States alters non-Asian Americans’ attitudes toward and knowledge about Asian Americans. Our findings suggest that historical and emotionally resonant messaging common in museum exhibits improves attitudes and beliefs, primarily among individuals who already hold positive racial predispositions. These results highlight both the promise and limits of museum-based interventions aimed at reducing out-group bias and speak to the efficacy of scalable digital interventions.

Commonality or Difference? Sexual Assault and the Foundations of Women's Solidarities in the United States

Abstract

How does emphasizing differences within a social group affect group solidarity and political behavior? I examine this question in an online survey experiment fielded amid the 2020 racial justice protests following the killing of George Floyd, a period in which racial inequality and the politics of policing were unusually salient. The experiment varies whether sexual assault is framed as an issue affecting women generally or women of color specifically. Both frames increase women’s solidarity, contrary to the expectation that emphasizing cross-cutting racial identities fragments group cohesion. Yet they generate distinct forms of solidarity. The gender-only frame activates what I call prototypical solidarity, while the intersectional frame increases feelings of commonality with and closeness to women of color among both White women and women of color, producing intersectional solidarity. The two frames also have different political consequences: the gender-only frame increases engagement with a Violence Against Women Act petition, an effect driven primarily by White women, whereas the intersectional frame increases support for racial and gender descriptive representation but not engagement with the VAWA petition. These findings show that intragroup differences need not undermine political solidarity, but can shape its content and political consequences. More broadly, the political significance of intersectionality is not simply that people possess multiple identities: invoking intragroup difference can change the content of perceived commonality without weakening identification with the broader group.

Rooted in Rights: Women's Bargaining Power on Forest Land
With Bhumi Purohit and Rashi Sabherwal. Funding: ETH4D Development Challenge Pilot Grant (2026–2028, 18 months)

Project Description

India's Forest Rights Act of 2006 (FRA) is the first law in India to mandate joint titling of forest land for married couples, marking a landmark provision for women's matrimonial property rights. Theoretically, formally conferred joint rights can, if effectively exercised, expand exit options and economic security for women, strengthening their position within the household. Yet, if joint rights are accompanied by social resistance and backlash from men or women of other generations, they reduce women's bargaining power. We examine the impact of joint property rights on women’s bargaining position within the household by testing the effects of an intervention that provides women with targeted legal information and paralegal facilitation to increase their uptake of joint forest land titles under the FRA. In partnership with the Working Group for Women and Land Ownership (WGWLO), a grassroots-based network with over two decades of experience advancing women's land rights, we implement a pilot randomized controlled trial. Treated gram panchayats receive a paralegal intervention: women obtain targeted information about their FRA rights and facilitated support to file joint titling claims. We survey women at baseline and endline to assess effects on titling claims, legal consciousness, and intra-household bargaining. Alongside the randomized controlled trial (RCT) survey, we collect administrative data on FRA title distributions to understand how claims are processed through the committees set up by the FRA, satellite data on forest cover and conduct qualitative interviews to understand mechanisms.

A Room of One's Own: Physical Infrastructure, Identity, and Access to Justice in Rural North India
With Anjali Thomas and Debabrata Samanta. Fieldwork beginning in November 2026.

Project Description

Why do some local state institutions become accessible and effective while others remain largely on paper? This project studies Bihar’s Gram Kachaharis—elected village-level courts operating under a common formal framework—to examine how institutional capacity shapes access to justice. We distinguish between the capacity of individual officeholders and the organizational capacity embedded in institutions themselves, asking how physical infrastructure, administrative support, access to other local institutions, and the social relationships of local officials affect who brings disputes, how cases are processed, and whether decisions are accepted and enforced. Combining administrative and spatial data with elite shadowing, the project examines when formal authority becomes meaningful state capacity—and whether stronger local institutions expand access to justice for women and marginalized communities.
